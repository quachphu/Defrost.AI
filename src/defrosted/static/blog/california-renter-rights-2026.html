California renter rights in 2026, in plain English

[[SAMPLE POST — review before launch. Verify every statutory specific against current law before publishing; statute numbers and figures change. Not legal advice.]]

California gives renters more protection than most states — but the protections only help if you know their shape. This is the plain-English map. For the specifics (which change, and which vary by city), use our state law explorer and the official sources linked at the end, and when it matters, talk to a lawyer or a local tenant-rights organization.

Your deposit isn't a tip

California limits how much a landlord can collect as a security deposit and puts a deadline on returning it after you move out, with an itemized accounting of anything withheld. If deductions show up that were never documented, the law is on your side. [[verify current deposit cap + return deadline before publish]]

Notice before entry, notice before changes

Your home is yours even though someone else owns it. Outside genuine emergencies, a landlord must give you advance written notice before entering, and rent increases and terminations come with their own minimum notice periods — longer ones for bigger changes. [[verify current notice periods]]

Habitability is not optional

Heat, water, working plumbing and wiring, a roof that keeps rain out — California's implied warranty of habitability means a unit has to be livable, and staying livable is the landlord's job. There are formal paths (with strict rules — follow them carefully) when repairs don't happen.

Rent caps and just-cause, for many renters

Statewide law caps annual rent increases and requires "just cause" for many evictions in covered buildings, and cities like Los Angeles and San Francisco layer stronger local rules on top. Whether you're covered depends on the building's age and type — worth checking before you assume either way. [[verify current AB 1482 cap formula + coverage]]

Retaliation is illegal

Complaining about a broken heater can't legally cost you your lease. Landlords may not retaliate against tenants for exercising their rights in good faith — and fair-housing law separately protects you from discrimination in every part of the process.
